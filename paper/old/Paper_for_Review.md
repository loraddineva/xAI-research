## Title (working)

*Automated Detection of Hallucination in LLM-Generated SHAP Narratives*

---

## Abstract

LLMs are increasingly used to translate SHAP feature-attribution values into natural-language explanations for tabular model predictions, yet the faithfulness of these narratives to the underlying attribution values has not been systematically measured. This paper operationalises four classes of faithfulness failure (sign inversion, rank swap, feature fabrication, and omission) as an automated evaluation pipeline that uses a separate LLM extraction model to parse narrative claims and compare the structured output to ground-truth SHAP values. We apply the pipeline to 300 narratives generated by crossing two prompt strategies (direct and chain-of-thought) with 150 instances from the Adult Income benchmark (OpenXAI; Agarwal et al., 2022), using Llama 3 70B for generation and Mistral 7B Instruct v0.3 for extraction. Three quarters of valid narratives contain at least one hallucination type (75.0%, 95% CI: 69.5–79.8%). Rank swap, the model emphasising the wrong features as most important, is the dominant failure at 68.8%. Chain-of-thought prompting reduces the overall rate to 58.5% and nearly eliminates sign inversion (4.2%), but leaves rank swap substantially unaddressed (57.7%). Feature fabrication is absent under both strategies. These results establish a baseline for faithfulness measurement in narrative XAI and provide a reusable open-source evaluation framework.

## 1. Introduction

Machine learning models are now deployed in settings where predictions must be explained to the people they affect. Credit applicants, patients, and people subject to automated employment or benefits decisions have a reasonable interest in knowing which factors drove the model's output. Shapley Additive Explanations, or SHAP (Lundberg & Lee, 2017), assign each input feature a signed numeric contribution that reflects how much it pushed the prediction toward or away from the positive class. Raw attribution tables are not easy for non-expert users to interpret [CITATION NEEDED], and large language models are now used to convert them into natural-language narratives [CITATION NEEDED].

The narratives work. Martens et al. (2024) showed that users who received LLM-generated SHAP narratives for income classification predictions answered comprehension questions significantly more accurately than users who received the attribution table alone, and more than 90% of participants rated the narratives as convincing. A narrative that reads as credible and improves comprehension is one users are likely to trust. If that narrative misrepresents the underlying attribution values, the persuasiveness becomes the hazard rather than the benefit. An inaccurate account that reads as plausible is more likely to be accepted, acted upon, and never questioned than one that reads as confused or incomplete. The more appealing the narrative form, the more consequential its inaccuracy.

Whether LLM-generated SHAP narratives are faithful to the attribution values they draw from has not been measured. Ichmoukamedov et al. (2024) proposed an automated evaluation pipeline and identified hallucination cases through manual analysis of a subsample, but did not report systematic rates and did not vary prompt design. No prior study systematically measures rates of each failure type across a full evaluation set or treats prompt strategy as an experimental variable.

This paper tests whether Llama 3 70B generates faithful SHAP narratives. We compare two prompt strategies, direct and chain-of-thought, across 150 instances from the Adult Income benchmark (OpenXAI, Agarwal et al., 2022), producing 300 narratives in total. We apply and operationalise four classes of faithfulness failure, grounded in the NLG hallucination literature, as a detection pipeline that compares extracted narrative claims to ground-truth SHAP values. Three quarters of valid narratives contain at least one faithfulness failure. Rank swap is the dominant type, present in 68.8% of valid narratives and in 57.7% even under chain-of-thought prompting. 

Section 2 reviews related work on hallucination in LLMs, narrative XAI, and faithfulness measurement. Section 3 describes the dataset, models, and prompt strategies. Section 4 defines the hallucination classes and detection procedure. Section 5 presents the results. Section 6 discusses the findings, states limitations, and concludes.

## 2. Related Work

Hallucinations in LLMs broadly divide into two categories. Factual hallucination describes outputs that contradict world knowledge. Faithfulness hallucination describes outputs that contradict a provided grounding source (Ji et al., 2023; Maynez et al., 2020). This paper concerns the latter exclusively. The SHAP table is the complete grounding source, every claim the narrative makes is checkable against it, and world knowledge is irrelevant.

Agarwal, Tanneru and Lakkaraju (2024) identify a structural tension in LLM explanation generation. RLHF training optimises for outputs that appear coherent to human evaluators, not for outputs that correspond to a grounding source. A plausible explanation and a faithful one are not the same thing, and in high-stakes settings the divergence has direct consequences. Turpin et al. (2023) make a complementary point from a different direction. Chain-of-thought explanations can be systematically unfaithful to a model's actual decision process. The reasoning is logically coherent but rationalises the output post-hoc. In SHAP narration there is no LLM decision process to rationalise. The faithfulness question is whether the narrative correctly relays a numeric table produced by a separate classical model.

### 2.2 Narrative XAI: from SHAP values to prose

SHAP (Lundberg & Lee, 2017) is the standard post-hoc attribution method for tabular predictions, with established applications in credit scoring, clinical decision support, and algorithmic auditing [CITATION NEEDED]. Its output is a signed numeric score for each feature, precise but technically dense. Studies of XAI comprehension by lay users consistently find that raw attribution tables are difficult to interpret without statistical training [CITATION NEEDED]. Natural-language generation offers a communication layer that converts attribution outputs into prose [CITATION NEEDED].

Martens et al. (2024) introduced XAIstories, LLM-generated narratives grounded in SHAP values or counterfactuals for tabular classification predictions. In a user study, over 90% of participants rated the narratives as convincing, and users who received narratives answered comprehension questions significantly more accurately than users who received raw SHAP tables [CITATION NEEDED]. The study did not measure whether the narratives accurately represented the SHAP values they were generated from. Ichmoukamedov, Hinns and Martens (2024) proposed automated metrics for XAI narrative evaluation using a two-LLM pipeline. A generation model produces a narrative from a SHAP table, and an extraction model parses the narrative into structured feature claims, which are compared against the SHAP input. Manual analysis on a subsample identified hallucination cases but rates are not reported systematically, and prompt strategy is not treated as an experimental variable. No prior study systematically measures rates of each failure type across a full evaluation set, varies prompt strategy as an experimental variable, or applies automated detection to SHAP narratives at scale.

### 2.3 Measuring faithfulness in LLM explanations

Two recent studies measure SHAP-adjacent faithfulness in LLM-generated explanations, but in a setup that differs from this paper in a consequential way. AlMarri et al. (2025) evaluated four open-source LLMs as zero-shot classifiers on financial tabular data, computed SHAP values over the LLMs' own predictions, and compared those SHAP values to the LLMs' self-reported feature impacts. Sign agreement between self-explanation and SHAP reached only 50 to 60% even for the most important features. Kendall's τ alignment with LightGBM SHAP was near zero [CITATION NEEDED]. Matton et al. (2025) defined causal concept faithfulness as the alignment between the concepts an LLM explanation implies are influential and those that causally affect model outputs, measured via counterfactual inputs and a Bayesian hierarchical model. GPT-3.5 explanations systematically over-cited behaviour-related concepts and omitted identity-related ones regardless of their causal relevance. In both studies, the LLM is the model being explained and also the source of the explanation. This paper asks whether LLMs faithfully relay a numeric grounding source produced by a separate classical model. The ground truth is fully external, the generation model has no stake in the explanation, and every directional claim is verifiable without causal inference.

Whether chain-of-thought prompting reduces faithfulness failures in SHAP narratives is an open empirical question. Wei et al. (2022) showed that chain-of-thought improves structured reasoning on multi-step tasks. Turpin et al. (2023) showed it can simultaneously surface reasoning that is coherent but unfaithful to the model's actual computation. In SHAP narration, neither outcome can be assumed. We test chain-of-thought as a treatment variable, not as a solution.

## 4. Hallucination Taxonomy and Detection

### 4.1 Taxonomy overview

The four failure types map onto a structural distinction. Sign inversion and rank swap are attribution errors, in which the narrative misrepresents a feature present in the source by assigning it the wrong direction or by displacing it from the set of most important features. Feature fabrication is an invention error, in which the narrative mentions content with no counterpart in the grounding source. Omission is a coverage error, in which the narrative fails to include source content that is important. This structure divides the types by whether the error is one of commission (attributing incorrectly or inventing) or of non-coverage (failing to relay what the source shows). Types are mutually non-exclusive and a single narrative may exhibit more than one. Relative effect size is not treated as a separate type. Detecting whether a narrative's implied magnitude is proportional to the SHAP value would require a graded scale rather than a binary flag and falls outside the scope of this paper.


| Type                | Definition                                                                                                     | Detection summary                                       |
| ------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Sign inversion      | Narrative states the wrong directional effect of a feature (positive vs. negative class)                       | Extracted `sign` vs. sign of SHAP value                 |
| Rank swap           | The set of top-*k* extracted features (by narrative `rank`) does not match the top-*k* SHAP features by |SHAP| | Compare top-*k* sets, ordering within set ignored       |
| Feature fabrication | Narrative mentions a feature not present in the dataset's feature set                                          | Non-empty `unknown_features` list in extraction JSON    |
| Omission            | At least one top-*k* SHAP feature (*k* = 3 by default) is absent from the narrative                            | Top-*k* SHAP features absent from extraction `features` |


### 4.2 Detection procedure

For each narrative, the extraction model (Mistral 7B Instruct v0.3) receives the narrative text, the full feature list for the dataset, and the prediction task description. It returns a JSON object in which each feature mentioned in the narrative is recorded with an importance rank (0 = most important), a direction (`sign`: 1 or −1), the feature value if explicitly stated, and a one-sentence causal assumption. A separate field, `unknown_features`, lists any feature-like names that do not match the valid feature set. A rule-based, deterministic comparison step checks each extracted field against ground-truth SHAP values and sets four binary flags. A narrative is marked `any_hallucination = 1` if at least one flag is set. Parse failures and ambiguous extractions are logged for manual review.

The omission rule is parameterised by *k* (default *k* = 3), set in `config/default.yaml`. Rank swap uses a set-based criterion rather than a strict ordering. The flag is raised when the set of *k* features assigned the lowest rank values by the extraction model differs from the set of *k* features with the largest |SHAP| values. Internal ordering within that set is ignored. This design distinguishes set-membership errors, in which the model emphasises the wrong features altogether, from ordering noise, in which the narrative discusses the correct top features in a different sequence than SHAP magnitude order. Human-in-the-loop validation against a gold-standard label set is planned as future work.

### 4.3 Extraction robustness check

Hallucination flags depend on the extraction model parsing the narrative correctly. To quantify extraction reliability independently of narrative faithfulness, we adapt the semantic uncertainty approach of Kuhn et al. (2023): for each narrative, the extraction prompt is submitted five times at temperature 0.9. Each run returns the same JSON schema as the main evaluation. For every feature mentioned in a majority of runs, we compute sign agreement as the proportion of runs that return the plurality sign (e.g., a 3/2 split yields 0.6). Value agreement is computed only when at least one run extracts a non-null numeric value. Top-*k* set agreement scores the fraction of runs whose top-*k* feature set matches the plurality set, ignoring internal ordering. The narrative reliability score is the mean of all per-feature sign agreements and the top-*k* set agreement; narratives scoring below 0.8 are flagged as low-reliability, and those with fewer than three successful parses are marked extraction-unreliable.

One sentence is enough. The "narrative reliability score" definition (mean of all sign agreements plus top-k agreement) should be stated as a formula or at least clarified: does "plus" mean concatenation before averaging, or addition of two separate means? Currently ambiguous — state it as "the mean of all sign-agreement values and the top-*k* set agreement value pooled together" or use notation.]

The check multiplies extraction API cost by five; for cost-constrained settings, calibrating the reliability distribution on a 10% subsample is sufficient to estimate how much of the corpus is robustly extractable.

### 5.3 Prompt strategy effects

Chain-of-thought prompting reduces overall hallucination substantially relative to direct: 58.5% versus 93.1% (χ²(1) = 43.40, *p* < .001). Rank swap also falls — from 80.8% to 57.7% (χ²(1) = 16.74, *p* < .001) — but it remains the dominant failure mode under both strategies. Two hallucination types show no significant difference between strategies: feature fabrication is zero under both, and omission rates are nearly identical at 15.4% versus 19.0% (χ²(1) = 0.63, *n.s.*).

Capital-related features are substantially underrepresented: `capital_gain` appears in only 19.5% of narratives and `capital_loss` in 22.1%, despite their often-large SHAP magnitudes. Narratives that skip capital features in favour of socially prominent ones such as sex, race, and marital status necessarily misidentify the top-*k* drivers — a direct contribution to rank swap and omission rates. No narrative references a feature name outside the valid feature list, confirming the zero fabrication rate.

> [*Editorial:* The observation about capital features is the most interesting finding in this section and the paper is right to foreground it. However, the claim "despite their often-large SHAP magnitudes" is asserted but not shown — include a supplementary table of mean |SHAP| per feature (planned for Appendix B) and cross-reference it here. Without it, a reviewer might ask whether capital features really do have large SHAP magnitudes in this sample. "Socially prominent" is an interpretive term — in a results section, prefer a neutral descriptor: "...in favour of features with clear demographic interpretations (sex, race, marital status)." Move "socially prominent" to §6.1.]

### 5.6 Extraction robustness

The robustness check was run on 68 narratives (five repeated extractions at temperature 0.9). One narrative yielded fewer than three successful parses and is excluded from reliability scoring. No narrative scored below the 0.8 reliability threshold. The mean narrative reliability score across the 67 scoreable narratives is 0.946 (SD = 0.049), indicating that the extraction model's sign assignments and feature-membership decisions are largely consistent across repeated runs.

> [*Editorial:* Clear and correctly concise. One question the paragraph does not answer: how was the 68-narrative subsample selected? Was it random, stratified by strategy, or a convenience sample? If random, state the seed. If stratified, state the allocation. This is a replication-relevant detail that is currently missing.]

Chain-of-thought narratives are extracted more consistently than direct narratives. The gap is sharpest for top-*k* set agreement: direct achieves a mean of 0.525 (SD = 0.226) versus 0.903 (SD = 0.157) for chain-of-thought. For direct narratives, repeated independent extractions agree on which three features the narrative treats as most important only about half the time, compared to nine times in ten for chain-of-thought. Rank swap flags on direct narratives are therefore a lower bound on true rank swap prevalence: the extraction model's uncertainty about which features the direct narrative emphasises means some flags may reflect extraction noise. Per-feature sign agreement is high for all features (minimum 0.935 for `relationship_Non_Husband`), so sign inversion flags are reliable under both strategies.

> [*Editorial:* This paragraph contains an important interpretive point — direct rank swap rates should be read as conservative — and it is stated clearly. The claim "lower bound on true rank swap prevalence" is precise: extraction noise would cause false negatives (missed rank swaps) more than false positives (spurious flags), because an ambiguous narrative is more likely to be inconsistently extracted than a clear one is to be consistently misflagged. This logic is correct but worth one additional sentence making the direction of bias explicit for readers less familiar with measurement error. Consider: "Ambiguous narratives, which are harder to extract consistently, are more likely to generate false negatives (missed rank swaps) than false positives."]

## 6. Discussion and Conclusion

### 6.1 Interpretation

The results separate two qualitatively different failure modes. Sign inversion is a local, within-feature error: the model discusses the right feature but assigns it the wrong direction. Chain-of-thought's explicit verification step — requiring the model to state the top feature and confirm its sign before writing the narrative — reduces this error from 60.0% to 4.2%. The intervention targets a specific, verifiable piece of information at generation time, and it works.

> [*Editorial:* The short final sentence — "and it works" — is punchy and appropriate here. The paragraph correctly attributes the success of chain-of-thought to a specific mechanism (the sign-verification step) rather than to chain-of-thought reasoning in general. One precision issue: the paragraph says the model must "confirm its sign" — more precisely, it must "state the top feature and verify its sign before writing." The current phrasing implies the model checks its own output, whereas the prompt structure requires it to produce the verification as a reasoning step before generating the narrative. Clarify the direction of causality.]

Rank swap is a different kind of failure and is not resolved by chain-of-thought prompting. It concerns which features the model treats as the most influential drivers of the prediction, not how it characterises any individual feature. Chain-of-thought reduces rank swap from 80.8% to 57.7%, but 57.7% remains a majority of narratives. The feature-mention data offers a partial explanation: `capital_gain` and `capital_loss` appear in fewer than a quarter of narratives despite their often-large SHAP magnitudes, while `hours_per_week` and `age` appear in almost every narrative regardless of strategy. The model appears to weight features by their interpretive salience — age and hours worked are easy to incorporate into a plausible income narrative — rather than by their SHAP magnitude [CITATION NEEDED: cite evidence that LLMs weight socially interpretable features more heavily in generation, or qualify as a hypothesis if no direct citation exists]. Chain-of-thought's ranking step partially corrects this: it forces the model to acknowledge which features rank highest by SHAP before writing, but the final narrative still underrepresents capital features. The co-occurrence data is consistent with this account: rank swap and sign inversion co-occur in 22.4% of narratives, suggesting the model has assigned attributions to incorrect features, simultaneously displacing the true top drivers and inverting direction.

> [*Editorial:* The paragraph is substantive and the capital-gain/capital-loss observation is the paper's most original finding. However, two claims need treatment. First, "The model appears to weight features by their interpretive salience" is an inference, not a result — flag it with "appears to" (already there) and consider adding "This remains a hypothesis; testing it would require systematically varying which features have large SHAP values while controlling for interpretive salience." Second, [CITATION NEEDED] is correctly flagged — there is a growing literature on model biases toward salient features in generation; if you cannot find a direct citation, state the hypothesis explicitly and leave its test to future work. The co-occurrence observation in the last sentence is a result, not an interpretation, and properly belongs in §5.4 — move it there and reference it here with "As the co-occurrence data show (§5.4)..."]

Feature fabrication is absent throughout: Llama 3 70B never introduces a feature name outside the valid set, even in the direct condition where it receives no structured reasoning scaffold. This suggests the feature list in the prompt functions as a hard constraint on vocabulary. The model's failures are in attribution — assigning effects to the wrong features or in the wrong direction — not in invention.

> [*Editorial:* Clean and precise. The contrast "attribution, not invention" is a useful formulation for the abstract or conclusion. The hypothesis that the feature list "functions as a hard constraint on vocabulary" is reasonable but not directly tested — the model could in principle mention a feature-adjacent synonym that the extraction model maps to a different name. Flag this as an assumption: "The feature list in the prompt appears to function as a constraint on vocabulary; no synonyms or paraphrases of feature names were detected in the `unknown_features` field."]

Omission is strategy-invariant: 15.4% versus 19.0%, with no statistically significant difference (χ²(1) = 0.63, *n.s.*). Chain-of-thought's explicit ranking step identifies the top-five features by |SHAP| but does not guarantee they all appear in the final narrative. Whether omission reflects a systematic bias against specific feature types — capital gains and losses are mentioned least, as §5.5 shows — or is a random generation artefact is a question for future work.

> [*Editorial:* Correct and appropriately brief. The cross-reference to §5.5 connects the omission result to the feature-mention data, which is the right analytic move. One addition: since chain-of-thought uses `max_tokens = 1024` versus 2,048 for direct, and the omission rate is slightly higher for chain-of-thought (19.0% vs. 15.4%, though not significantly so), a sentence noting that length constraints may contribute to omission in the chain-of-thought condition is worth including — even if the difference is not significant, the directional trend and the mechanism are relevant disclosures.]

### 6.2 Limitations

**Detection coverage.** Extraction errors propagate to hallucination flags: a feature mentioned but mis-parsed as a different feature would generate a false sign inversion or rank swap flag. Precision and recall against a human-labelled gold standard have not been measured. The robustness check (§4.3) partially addresses this by identifying narratives where repeated high-temperature extractions disagree; low-reliability flags should not be treated as definitive without manual review.

> [*Editorial:* Correctly stated. Add the practical implication: "The rank swap rates for direct narratives, in particular, should be read as lower bounds, given that top-*k* set agreement for that strategy is 0.525 — indicating extraction uncertainty is high." This connects the §5.6 finding back to the limitations, which is where a reader would look for this caveat.]

**Dataset and model scope.** Results are specific to Adult Income, Llama 3 70B for generation, and Mistral 7B Instruct v0.3 for extraction, both at temperature 0.0. Results may not generalise to other tabular domains (e.g., credit risk, clinical prediction), non-linear model classes (e.g., gradient boosting, neural networks), other LLM families, or higher generation temperatures. The logistic regression model from OpenXAI produces SHAP values of moderate variance across instances; a model with more extreme attribution distributions might produce different hallucination patterns.

> [*Editorial:* Good. The note about the logistic regression model's attribution variance is a genuine insight — it is not a standard limitation statement, it flags a specific mechanism by which results might differ. The temperature limitation is correctly included. One addition: the asymmetry in model size between generation (70B) and extraction (7B) is a limitation — a more capable extraction model might recover feature claims that Mistral 7B misses, which would affect the hallucination rate estimates.]

**Sample size.** 150 instances is sufficient to detect the large differences observed — sign inversion at 60.0% vs. 4.2% would be detectable with far fewer — but small per-type differences between strategies (e.g., omission at 15.4% vs. 19.0%) are likely underpowered. The 28 parse failures (9.3%) reduce effective sample sizes and introduce missing data that is not uniformly distributed across strategies.

> [*Editorial:* Good and specific. The note that the observed large effects are not a power problem is correctly included — reviewers sometimes question small samples reflexively, and pre-empting this is useful. The parse failure note is important and correctly flags that the missing data may not be MCAR (missing completely at random). Consider whether to report any sensitivity analysis showing that results are stable if parse failures are assigned to the "hallucinated" or "not hallucinated" category.]

**Generation length.** direct narratives allow up to 2,048 tokens; chain-of-thought narratives are capped at 1,024. The slight directional difference in omission rates between strategies (15.4% direct vs. 19.0% chain-of-thought) is not statistically significant, but a token budget that halves the available length cannot be ruled out as a contributing factor. Future work comparing strategies at matched token budgets would resolve this.

> [*Editorial:* Correct to include this and to note the non-significance. The limitation is real and clearly bounded — the current analysis cannot separate "chain-of-thought caused omission" from "shorter token budget caused omission." The suggestion for matched-budget comparison is a concrete and testable future direction, which is exactly what a limitations section should offer.]

### 6.3 Conclusion

LLMs do not reliably report what SHAP values show. Across 272 valid evaluations of Llama 3 70B narratives on the Adult Income benchmark, 75.0% contain at least one faithfulness failure. Rank swap, emphasising the wrong features as the most important drivers, affects 68.8% of narratives overall and 57.7% even under chain-of-thought prompting. Feature fabrication is absent under both strategies. Chain-of-thought nearly eliminates directional errors (sign inversion at 4.2%), but the problem of feature selection fidelity is not resolved by prompting alone.

Faithfulness cannot be assumed. Users of LLM-generated SHAP narratives, in credit decisions, clinical support, or audit contexts, may be reading explanations that emphasise different features than the model actually weighted most highly. Automated verification of the kind described here is tractable and should accompany any deployment that uses LLMs to narrate feature attributions. The detection framework is released as an open-source resource [CITATION NEEDED: add repository URL or anonymous link before submission].

This paper provides the first study to systematically measure hallucination rates at scale across a full evaluation set, vary prompt strategy as an experimental variable, and apply automated detection to SHAP narratives. Papers 2 and 3 of this thesis address the human consequences of each hallucination type and the conditions under which users detect or accept faithfulness failures.
